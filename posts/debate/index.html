She heard it clearly, despite the studio lights frying the edges of her vision. Despite the glassy gleam of a thousand pupils reflecting off her pearl-buttoned blazer and her blood-red lipstick smile.

Преступница в маске реформатора. (개혁가의 탈을 쓴 범죄자)

Somewhere in the crowd, a muffled intake of breath. Fingernails clacked against clipboard plastic. One of the opposition's handlers shifted audibly in his chair. Vadim, seated grim and slow-breathing behind the stage camera, flicked his cigarette ash into an empty vodka glass like a funeral bell.

Her lashes didn't even flicker.

Roza uncrossed one leg and let it stretch out slightly under the polished conference table, a deliberate ease, like a lioness shifting her weight in tall grass. The moderator made some pathetic clearing of throat, unsure whether to intervene after such a savage provocation on live national television. But she leaned into her mic before he could make up his mind.

"Ah," she murmured in that coiled-rich timbre of hers, the edge of a Moscow patrician and a girl who knew where corpses were buried, "Young Comrade Yaroshenko is very fond of dramatic phrases. As if we are debating not in civil society, but in some samizdat fantasy novel for angry boys."

The audience chuckled. Not all of it, but enough. The kind of laugh that came when people wanted blood and were almost embarrassed to admit it.

She turned her head then—not just the face, but the whole angle of her spine—to regard her accuser. Her gray-blue eyes didn't blink. They slid over him as if measuring what part of him was backbone and what part was just leftover rot from the Zone. No overt scorn, not yet. That was for amateurs.

"You ask about my campaign financing. Of course you do. When there is no vision of your own to offer the people, attack the power of someone who has it, da?" Her accent thickened purposefully on that last syllable, slicing through the silence.

She reached for the glass of ice water beside her, lifted it without sound, letting the condensation trail down her fingers. Sipped. Set it back down.

"Let me simplify this answer for our viewers, many of whom did not spend the last five years filing petitions from a hallway in Serpukhov." Her eyes snapped again. "I am transparent. My financial disclosures are filed, vetted, and available in my legal campaign office—as is standard for all candidates serious about winning, and not simply performing grief theater for the cameras."

The blow landed cleaner than a slap. Someone in the back choked on something between a cough and a laugh. Roza didn't pause to allow the sound dignity.

"As for the charming insult," she said with a gaze now narrowing, her smile gone surgical, "a criminal in the mask of a reformer… Allow me to reframe it in language more honest to this land."

She leaned forward just half an inch, causing her lapel to cast a shadow across her chest. The overhead lights caught on the small mole below her collarbone.

"Men like you see women like me and think, She does not belong here. She must have cheated. Slept. Killed. Wriggled her way in. You stand behind a certificate, waving it like incense, and believe that makes you saint enough to curse at me. But I'll tell you this, Comrade Chuzhak."

Her eyes dropped to the desk for a second. Her next sentence was so slow, it came out like smoke dragging through velvet drapes.

"If I were a criminal… you'd be bleeding out on this stage already."

The moderator blanched. A stunned murmur broke through the studio. Vadim folded his arms behind the camera, unmoving.

But Roza smiled again, like a duchess offering dessert.

"Fortunately for you," she cooed, sitting back again, arms folded with exquisite control, "I am simply a better candidate."

She tapped her fingernail once, just once, against the microphone stem. Then silence. The cameras drank it in.

She didn't blink once through the entire tirade. Not even when the word sold dropped with the deliberate contempt of a hammer wrapped in velvet.

Roza sat as though the chair itself belonged to her spine. Her fingers rested lightly on the edge of the table, unmoving, the glossy white polish catching the studio lights like porcelain. Across the room, Vadim's jaw flexed once, but didn't open. Good dog.

The moment Yaroshenko turned away from her to address the moderator, she allowed herself the smallest movement: a smile that peeled across her mouth like the edge of a wound. He had given her a gift. Finally.

When the boy finished, the applause was cautious. Some sympathetic murmurs near the rear rows. A few young journalists scribbling eagerly, sensing a headline. But the silence that followed it all? That was hers. Thick as evening frost and just as unforgiving.

She turned again, slow as ritual. Tilted her head just slightly.

"Hmm." A soft, feline hum, far more dangerous than anything she'd said before.

"Khorosho." Her tone was disarmingly mild, like a disappointed teacher. She reached again for her water, dragged her fingers over the ice-sweating glass, and then didn't lift it. Just let her hand rest on its side as if warming herself on the chill of it.

"Comrade Yaroshenko," Roza began, her voice syruped with grace so unnatural it scraped, "I do admire the way you speak of women's rights. It's touching. Especially from a man whose followers deface campaign posters of female candidates with crude illustrations. Shall I show the audience the photos from Solntsevo station?"

A stir swept the room. Roza didn't need evidence. She only needed confidence—and that ominous, clinical certainty in her voice that made people believe the bones had already been dug from the ground.

"There's something very Soviet in your little righteousness," she mused, almost dreamily now, making eye contact only at intervals. "So concerned with what someone once was. So afraid of what a woman might become."

She adjusted in her seat, this time letting her elbow rest on the table and her fingers touch the edge of her chin, eyes narrowed but half-lidded, the kind of gaze that could sear straight through a man and forget he had ever been alive to begin with.

"I'll be honest, though," Roza continued, glancing to the audience as if she now pitied them, "I did wonder when you'd bring up the brothel lie. You lasted longer than I predicted. Congratulations."

There was no denial. There never was. Just the iron clasp of her amusement around his throat.

"The truth is, young man, if I had done half the crimes you claim, I'd already be sitting in the Federation Council with three oligarchs licking my heels and a minister's corpse in the Moskva River. But I suppose you've always been better at fantasy than power."

Another buzz moved through the chamber. This one sharper. The moderator looked exhausted. Someone in the front row grinned without quite meaning to.

Roza sat back once more and let her final words slip out calm as a dagger into snow.

"But since we are speaking of contradictions… allow me one more."

Now her eyes met his. Slowly. Directly.

"You speak with fury about the forgotten people of Chernobyl. And yet, you wield your grief like a child playing soldier. Shouting about strangers, about ghosts… while the living rot."

Her voice lowered further.

"You pity yourself so much, Chuzhak, you've started confusing it for justice."

Then, smiling sweetly as though she'd just settled a bill, she turned her eyes back toward the moderator and offered a nod of politeness so refined it glinted like surgical steel.

"I yield my time."

He doesn't know he is also a criminal. That's what makes his kind so predictable. They think pain is a halo. A blank permission slip. Lick your tragedy into a blade, and then act surprised when your enemies taste blood instead of tears.

She nodded, walking past him.

"I'll wear something darker," Roza murmured. "So the blood doesn't show."

Two days later, the heavy windows of Roza's Rublyovka mansion turned gold from the late-November sun bleeding through frost. The snow hadn't fallen yet, but the sky's color had gone the shade of old meat. She sat in the solarium, barefoot on warm stone tiles, one leg propped up on the cushioned arm of a leather chaise, absently flipping the pages of a back issue of Ogonyok with her thumb. A fine curl of smoke rose from a thin Davidoff between her fingers. Half a cigar wasted already. She hadn't taken a drag in minutes.

Vadim stood on the edge of the room, arms crossed, jacket open over a worn pistol holster. He waited in silence. She hated when he did that—it meant he didn't want to say what needed saying.

"Well?" Her voice was low but cutting. "Say it already, or cough and choke on it like an old bureaucrat."

"They've got nothing," Vadim said, scratching at his jaw. "No kompromat on your boy. Not even expired lunch receipts. No whisper of bribes, no shady donors we can twist. His financials are so clean you'd think he was laundering the floorboards in that shithole office of his."

Roza didn't respond. She turned a page, eyes not moving.

"He even returned a donation from one of the Serpukhov immigrants because the man had done time in '87 for stealing wood. Fucking firewood."

Still nothing.

Vadim took a breath, slower now. "If you want my view… this ain't a crusader who dirties his hands when nobody's looking. He's not corrupted. He's just… diseased. With righteousness. The kind that doesn't crack."

Roza dropped the magazine. It slid off her lap onto the tiled floor with a flutter.

"I don't need him to crack," she said softly.

Vadim exhaled, half a laugh. "You wanted to break him on television and you didn't. Now you can't even piss on his file. Want to make up a story?"

"No." She stood, brushing hair off her shoulder, the robe she wore falling open at the collar. Her naked skin beneath glowed white, translucent against the cold-filtered light behind her. She didn't bother tying it.

"I already explained the trick on stage," she muttered. "They'd be crawling over it now. Cameras on every wall. His rats report to each other like priests at confession."

"Correct." Vadim nodded. "You fucked yourself with your own clever mouth."

She rounded on him.

"No, I underestimated how dull saints can be." She was fully animated now, eyes narrowing into frost-cut glass. "He has no closet skeletons because he's been walking around with the coffin nailed shut on his back since he was sixteen."

"You admit you lost."

"I didn't lose," Roza said, voice sharp enough to flay.

And it wasn't even really anger. It was worse. Something deeper. Hunger curdling into irritation.

She paced three steps toward the glass doors, the sight of the pond beyond frozen shallow and still. The koi beneath didn't move. Dead or waiting, it was unclear.

Her lips twisted.

"A scarecrow held up by memory and cancer. All slogans and screams. And yet, there's nothing to club him with because he smells like truth."

Vadim tilted his head. "So, what? You let him parade around in rags while the polls tighten?"

"No." She turned back, eyes sharp, cruel with thought. "If I cannot sink him with lies, I will kill his image with something worse."

Vadim raised an eyebrow.

Roza's voice dropped to a near-whisper.

"Pity."

A pause. No movement between them. Only the silent drift of ash off the burned-out end of her cigar onto the marble below.

"I'll find something people can't stand in a saint," she said, barely audible now. "Even if I have to invent it in how I treat him."

She lit another cigar from the ember of the first and looked straight ahead at the empty space where nothing yet failed.

It was a diner steeped in decades of salted broth and human breath. A cracked ceramic bust of Lenin presided silently above the doorframe, and the glass at the counter housed a dying lemon slice inside a bottle of Tarkhuna. The radiator gasped under the winter-weight of scarfed shoulders and factory boots.

And then it fell dead quiet at three tables.

Roza Petrovna Zakharova stood still just beyond the swinging door, swathed in dark wool and trimmed sable, the high collar framing her neck like a threat. She hadn't taken off her gloves. Her gray-blue eyes scanned the space without moving her head, and only flicked once as if brushing past him—but not registering him—as if she were bored by the shape of a familiar mark in a crime scene photograph.

She approached the counter like she'd commissioned its marble two centuries ago.

"You sell vodka here?" she asked the cashier flatly.

The middle-aged woman behind the register blinked. "Not since the new ordinance, comrade. We've got cranberry mors, and tarhun."

Roza gave a sigh that came from somewhere very old in her chest. "Bozhe moy. Fine. Pour it. Three fingers. No foam."

A spoon clinked against porcelain behind her. She didn't react. Not even when an exhalation behind her shifted into something meant to provoke. She only turned, slow as frostbite, and finally looked straight at him.

At the table. That wretched table. That factory-tier plastic tray smirking with worker's meal symmetry. The way he smiled like he was beloved by gutters and ghosts alike.

She watched the food as he listed it, eyes dropping briefly to the pirozhok as though assessing if it could be weaponized. Then her gaze lifted and she met his eyes without blinking.

She let the silence stretch in unnerving comfort, then said low and flat:

"Hmm."

She stepped toward his table. Not hurriedly. Not quite slowly either. Just with the softness of intention one might find in circling a sick animal too proud to lie down.

"Comrade Yaroshenko," she began, removing one leather glove and placing it beside the napkin dispenser, "you misunderstand the nature of cruelty. True cruelty is not in poisoning a man's lunch…"

She reached for his cheburek without asking and lifted it delicately, as if inspecting a document for signature forgery.

"…It's in showing him how cheap his sense of victory tastes." She broke the pastry in half. Steam burst out in a quick breath.

"And still eating it."

Without ceremony, she bit into it.

The waitress at the kiosk window paused mid-step, eyes wide. A younger man at a nearby table muttered something and started whispering furiously to his friend.

Roza chewed once. Twice.

Then she spat the half-chewed dough into the ashtray on the metal tray beside him and wiped her mouth with the back of her gloved hand.

"How repulsive," she said, calmly. "But I expected that."

She dusted her fingers once, then pulled the glove back on with slow finality. One stretch per finger. As though suiting up for surgery.

Then, before leaving:

"No need to reveal your receipt, Chuzhak. You've already been priced."

She turned—not theatrically, just clean. And walked to the far end of the diner toward the front exit, where Vadim waited, leaning half in shadow, jaw tight with the kind of ire that wanted movement. She didn't glance back.

The door closed behind her with a hollow smack of wood and wind.

  • 체부레키를 뺏어서 씹어먹고 뱉은 뒤 로자가 한 말. 그야말로 탐미적으로 완벽해서 정석으론 뭘 어떻게 못 한다는 생각이 들었다. 사실 이 장면 로자의 모든 묘사-대사가 좋다.

  • “Pay up before you go, Comrade. My meal expenses come from the generous support of my comrades, not from funds meant to feed criminals.”

장면 짧은 소감

  • 이 즈음에서 로맨스 관련 태그를 싹 지우고 다시 썼다. 이렇게 재수없는 놈에게 반할리도 없을 뿐더러, 로자가 봐주는 것 없이 예브게니에게 살벌한 모습을 보였으면 했기 때문에.

  • 예브게니가 생각하는 자신과, 로자가 생각하는 예브게니에 대한 해석이 크게 어긋나는 것도 특징. 두 사람의 배경을 극명하게 드러낼 뿐만 아니라, 로자는 예브게니를 선천적으로 이해할 수 없다. 라는 느낌이 물씬 나서 좋았다.

  • 로자의 모든 장면은 느와르같고, 여유넘치고, 살벌하고 멋있고, 같은 장면에 나타나는 예브게니는 그런 로자를 “추악한 범죄자” 가 허세부리는 것처럼 격하시킨다. 예브게니는 부패한 자가 그렇게 허세부리는 것 자체를 역겨워하기 때문에. 아마 로자가 부패한 과거를 진심으로 참회하고 약자를 신경쓰는 사람이었다면 오히려 그런 모든 모습에 매력을 느꼈을지도 모른다.
